## Why this source matters

Dunford treats positioning as the deliberate context that makes a product's value understandable. The framework prevents copy teams from polishing a message before agreeing on what buyers compare, which attributes matter, who cares most, and which category frame makes those strengths obvious.

## Core concepts

- Positioning is an input to messaging and tactics, not a tagline.
- The true competitor may be a manual process, spreadsheet, inaction, or internal workaround—not the company listed on a battlecard.
- Features matter through the value they enable and the proof that supports that value.
- Best-fit customers are defined by why they care, not by broad demographic labels alone.
- A category frame activates expectations about alternatives, features, buying process, and price.
- Trends may create urgency and relevance, but should be layered on only when the product's value genuinely connects to them.

## Frameworks

### Five-plus-one positioning components

1. **Competitive alternatives:** what customers would use or do if the product did not exist.
2. **Unique attributes:** capabilities or delivery characteristics the real alternatives lack.
3. **Value and proof:** customer benefit enabled by those attributes, supported by evidence.
4. **Target-market characteristics:** observable conditions that make a buyer care strongly about that value.
5. **Market category:** the frame that helps buyers understand the product and its value.
6. **Relevant trend (optional):** a current shift that increases relevance without replacing the category.

Dependency order matters: alternatives → unique attributes → value → who cares → category → optional trend.

### Ten-step positioning process

1. Start with customers who strongly value the product.
2. Assemble a cross-functional positioning team.
3. Align vocabulary and suspend inherited positioning assumptions.
4. List true competitive alternatives.
5. Isolate attributes unique relative to those alternatives.
6. Map attributes to value themes and proof.
7. Identify who cares most and why.
8. Select a market frame that centers those strengths.
9. Layer on a relevant trend cautiously.
10. Capture the result so marketing, sales, and product can use it consistently.

## Diagnostic signals

- messaging leads with features whose comparative value is unexplained;
- the “competitor” list differs from what customers actually considered;
- category choice forces the funnel to fight incorrect expectations;
- target definition is demographic but lacks a reason for urgent fit;
- a trendy label appears without a credible product/value connection;
- price objections stem from an unfavorable comparison set;
- ads, sales calls, and product pages use inconsistent category language.

## Funnel and creative applications

- Before writing hooks or offers, build an evidence table: alternative → unique attribute → value → proof → best-fit condition.
- Use category language early enough to orient the prospect, then show the differentiated value.
- Create separate message variants only when customer context or alternatives genuinely differ.
- Treat “do nothing” as a competitor and address the cost/risk of status quo without inventing loss estimates.
- Align ad promise, landing-page category frame, demo narrative, and sales enablement.

## Questions this source helps answer

- Compared with what is this offer better?
- Which product attributes produce customer value?
- Who cares most, and what observable condition identifies them?
- Which category helps rather than harms understanding?
- Is a trend a useful relevance layer or empty decoration?

## Misuse risks and limitations

- The ten-step process is practitioner guidance, not a universal causal law.
- Starting from “customers who love the product” can introduce survivorship bias; supplement with lost-deal, churn, and non-customer research.
- A narrow best-fit focus can be useful for execution but should not automatically become the ceiling of the addressable market.
- A new category creates education cost and requires stronger evidence than a label change.
- Competitive alternatives and trends become stale; verify them in current market evidence.

## Key operational takeaways

- Establish the real comparison before writing the claim.
- Derive value from differentiated capability and evidence.
- Use category as a cognitive shortcut, not a fashionable label.
- Revalidate positioning when alternatives, product, or market conditions change.
